Re: Your Application

Six months, many emails, one reply

In this story

  • How my interest in research began
  • Where I searched for positions
  • Handling rejection
  • Building a research network
  • What applications asked for
  • Asking for feedback
  • The Marie Curie mobility rule
  • From offer to first day in Greece

Based on my experience applying for research positions in Europe. Requirements can differ in other countries.

I thought finding a PhD position would mostly be about finding the right project. I didn't realise it would also involve learning patience, handling rejection, and becoming very familiar with my inbox.

My search for a Marie Skłodowska-Curie doctoral position began during my Master's at Trinity College Dublin, around March 2025. I was approaching the end of my second semester and starting my dissertation, and I began thinking seriously about what would come next. I had always imagined becoming a professor someday, partly because I grew up in a family of teachers. But until then, research had been more of an interest than a clear career path.

My dissertation changed that. My professor introduced me to a PhD student who mentored me, and our conversations helped me understand what a research career could actually look like. I also remember visiting the research lab and finding it surprisingly peaceful. Seeing people work on problems they cared about made academia feel less like a distant ambition and more like a life I could build.

So I started looking. I explored university websites, LinkedIn, EURAXESS and research project pages, and applied to positions that matched my interests and background. I also started learning about Marie Curie doctoral positions and their eligibility requirements. There was a lot I didn't know at the beginning, and I learned most of it along the way.

Some applications led to interviews, and others led nowhere. One took me through eight interview rounds before the team chose another candidate, which was difficult to accept after investing so much time and effort. There were other rejections too. I had finished my coursework and received my marks, and most of my days were spent applying without knowing when something would work out. For a while, I questioned my abilities and wondered whether I should keep pursuing research or focus on finding a job.

It was a mentally exhausting period, but it taught me something important. A rejection after several successful interview rounds does not mean you lack potential. Sometimes another candidate is simply a closer fit for that particular role.

During this time, I made a conscious effort to build my network. I reached out to professors, introduced myself to researchers, attended hackathons and networking events, and kept track of potential supervisors, projects, deadlines and follow-ups in Notion. I learned to be proactive rather than wait for the right opportunity to find me.

One experience in particular stayed with me. After I was not selected for a position, a professor contacted me to say she saw potential in me and encouraged me to explore another research funding route. We stayed in touch while working towards that application. Through her, I saw that research goes beyond individual projects. It is also about building collaborations, supporting others and creating a community. She reminded me that I wanted to become a professor not only because I enjoy learning, but because I hope to help others learn and grow too. That application did not work out, but the experience stayed with me, and it taught me that the people you meet along the way matter, even when a particular opportunity doesn't.

The applications themselves needed careful preparation. Depending on the position, I needed a CV, a motivation letter, references, a research proposal and supporting documents. I drew on my Master's projects, my dissertation, my publications and my academic experience. My motivation letters also reflected something personal, my background in a family of teachers and my long-standing interest in education and academia. My references came from professors who knew my academic work, including my dissertation supervisor and other faculty members I had connected with during my Master's. Building those relationships in a one-year programme was not easy, which is why I would encourage anyone interested in research to start engaging with professors early.

I also learned to ask for feedback after rejections whenever possible. A polite message thanking the interviewers and asking what I could improve sometimes gave me useful insights, and it helped me keep a professional connection. Feedback is not always available, but it is always worth asking respectfully.

That habit ended up mattering more than I expected. I had found a Marie Curie position I was genuinely excited about, but I learned that its mobility rule made me ineligible for the Ireland-based route. Marie Curie positions generally require that you have not lived or worked in the host country for more than twelve months in the three years before starting, and my Master's year in Ireland counted. Instead of giving up on the project, I looked into its other locations and contacted the team to express my interest. I learned that a position in Patras, Greece, was still available, and that I could apply through the project directly.

I applied and interviewed with the professor, and we discussed my background, experience and research knowledge. A few days later, on 20 October 2025, I received the email offering me the position. I remember the date because it was Naraka Chaturdashi, part of the Diwali celebrations that are meaningful to my family and me, and receiving the news that day made the moment feel especially personal. After months of searching, applying, interviewing and questioning myself, I finally had the opportunity I had been hoping for.

Accepting it meant another big decision. By then, I had started building a life in Ireland, with friends, professional connections and a sense of familiarity that had taken time to develop. Moving to Greece meant starting over in a new country once again. But this opportunity was aligned with the future I wanted to build, so I decided to take it.

The months between the offer and my arrival involved contracts, document apostilles, visa applications, police clearance and the practical side of relocating. Some things took longer than expected, and others worked out because people were kind enough to help when I needed it. I joined the office in Patras as a Marie Curie doctoral candidate on 1 January 2026.

Looking back, my route here was not straightforward. There were applications that went nowhere, interviews that ended in disappointment, people who encouraged me, and an opportunity I found only because I kept looking beyond the first answer.

If you are beginning your own Marie Curie search, here is what I would share:

  • Get to know the eligibility criteria early. Mobility rules and other conditions decide whether you can apply, so it helps to understand them from the start.
  • Look beyond one platform. Explore EURAXESS, university websites, LinkedIn, research groups and project pages. Sometimes contacting a project directly can help you discover opportunities you might otherwise miss.
  • Build academic relationships early. Engage with professors during your Master's, especially if you are considering a research career. Their guidance and references can be valuable later.
  • Keep your applications organised. Track potential supervisors, deadlines, documents and follow-ups. A simple system makes a long search more manageable.
  • Ask for feedback and keep learning. Not every rejection comes with an explanation, but each interview is a chance to reflect, improve and prepare for the next one.

I am still learning, and still figuring out what kind of researcher I want to become. But I often think about that year. About every application I sent without knowing if anyone would read it, and every person who took the time to reply kindly anyway. Most of the emails that began with "Re: Your Application" said no. It only took one to say yes, and that one brought me here.

So if you are somewhere in the middle of your own search, refreshing your inbox and wondering whether it will ever work out, please keep going. Your yes might be just one email away.
