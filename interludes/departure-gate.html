Departure Gate

From a dream on Trinity's Instagram page to studying abroad in Ireland

For four years of my bachelor's, Trinity College Dublin had a very dedicated follower in India. It just didn't know about it.

Studying abroad was never only about getting a degree. I wanted to experience the life I had seen through university pages and student stories, to live in another country, become independent and build something for myself. At the same time, I had other options. I had a campus placement offer and had qualified GATE. But if Trinity worked out, I knew where I wanted to go.

In the end, choosing Ireland came down to practical things. A curriculum I liked, an English-speaking environment, a one-year Master's, a cost that was more manageable than some alternatives, and the chance to stay closer to home than I would in countries farther away. My advice is to start with your own priorities and budget, and then shortlist countries and universities that genuinely fit them.

Once I had decided, the preparation took almost a year. I spent six months preparing for the GRE, although I eventually didn't take the exam. That preparation strengthened my English enough that around two months of IELTS preparation was sufficient. I worked with a consultancy for my applications, but I would still encourage students to do their own research first. Understand the universities, courses, fees and requirements yourself. A consultancy can help with the process, but the decisions should be your own.

For my statement of purpose, I wrote about something that has stayed with me since childhood. I grew up in a family of teachers, and education has always been important to me. I wanted to keep learning and perhaps teach someday. I think connecting my background to what I wanted to do next made my application personal. My recommendation letters came from professors who knew my academic work and project experience well.

The timeline itself looked like this. I started IELTS preparation in October 2023, applied to Trinity in November, received a conditional offer in January 2024, and flew to Ireland on 2 September 2024. In between there were examinations, degree documents, finances and visa applications, so the process needed patience and planning. When the offer finally came, I was over the moon.

Leaving India was a strange feeling. I remember sitting by the window and watching the wheels lift off the ground. At some point I realised I had officially left. I knew I would return to visit, but I also knew my everyday life would now be somewhere else.

I landed in Dublin around 8 p.m., a little nervous about taking a taxi alone in a new country. The driver was kind, the accommodation staff helped me settle in, and things soon felt less intimidating. It also helped that I wasn't starting from zero. Trinity had connected incoming students through online groups and a pre-arrival meeting in Mumbai, and classmates who had arrived earlier helped me figure out supermarkets, restaurants and public transport. That early community made a real difference.

I stayed in student accommodation with my own room and a shared kitchen. It was secure and convenient, though expensive. That first year also taught me what to pack. Bring essentials such as medicines, familiar spices and electronics, but don't use luggage space on bulky jackets or bedding that you can buy locally. Check the weather, local shops and what your accommodation provides before deciding what to carry.

Daily life took some adjusting. As a vegetarian, I cooked for myself most of the time. Dublin's weather was often grey, and I had to learn to manage my own meals, transport and routine. Student transport discounts helped, and the Indian community was a real support. There were moments when I missed home, and calling my parents and spending time with friends helped. University also makes meeting people easier, with clubs, activities and events. You just have to be willing to show up.

Academically, Trinity was more practical than I expected. Beyond exams, there were assignments, group projects and tasks that required building something rather than only learning theory. A one-year Master's is intense, because you are adjusting to a new country while managing coursework, a dissertation and the pressure of thinking about your next step. It is manageable, but you need to work consistently. Outside class, I attended hackathons, networking events and other activities, which helped me explore interests beyond coursework and meet people outside my academic circle. I would encourage students to make time for these, even when the workload feels heavy.

I didn't take a part-time job during my Master's, so I can't offer firsthand advice on balancing work and study. My parents supported my education and preferred that I focus on the degree, and I was fortunate to have that. Every student's situation is different, so budget carefully, understand your funding and decide based on your own circumstances.

After graduation came the job search, which was challenging for me. I went through multiple interviews and often heard that the role had gone to candidates with more experience or different qualifications. My experience isn't a complete picture of the job market, but it taught me patience. I eventually received a job offer in Ireland, but by then I had decided to pursue a PhD.

That decision had started during my Master's. While working on my dissertation, I was guided by a PhD student who became a mentor to me, and seeing his work made me want to do research too. Through him, I learned about Marie Skłodowska-Curie doctoral positions and began looking for one that matched my interests. After months of applications and interviews, that path led me to my current research. That part is a story of its own.

Was studying abroad worth it? Absolutely.

It wasn't always easy. There were difficult decisions, some loneliness and moments of uncertainty. But it gave me more than a degree. It gave me independence, confidence, international experience and a clearer idea of the work I want to do.

If I could speak to my younger self at the departure gate, I would say, Go for it. You don't know exactly how the future will unfold, but you are capable of building more than you can imagine yet.

You don't need every step figured out before you begin. A direction and the willingness to prepare are a good place to start.
