Before et al.

On becoming a first author

  • From idea to results

  • Advice for a first paper

In citations, a paper is usually remembered by one name, followed by "et al." This is the story of the first time that name was mine. It took about four months, more than twenty papers, and one very long flight without Wi-Fi.

Even before I officially started on 1 January 2026, I knew which project I would be working on. In November, I spent time learning the basics of 6G, which was new to me. In December, while I was in Patras sorting out my residency, I met my co-supervisor. He explained what was expected of me, suggested I read up on the state-of-the-art approaches in the area, and shared material to start with. That material turned out to be incredibly useful.

So December and January were mostly reading. I went through around twenty to twenty-five papers, including review papers and state-of-the-art work, noting down my thoughts as I went. Honestly, I was anxious and probably overdoing it. But it gave me a map of the field, and if you are in that phase now, I would recommend starting with review papers. They save you a lot of time.

The real turning point came in mid-February, when I began working closely with a senior PhD researcher in our lab who guided my day-to-day research. Until then, I had been reading and taking notes on my own. Now we discussed papers together, and that changed everything. Reading helps you understand a paper, but discussing it with someone who knows the area as well as you do shows you what you don't know yet. It was in one of those discussions that we found the gap my work would address.

From there, the work was mine to do. The code for the state-of-the-art method I wanted to compare against didn't run at first, and it took time and patience to get it working. Then I implemented my own idea, and the early results were encouraging. I wrote the paper in about a month and a half, through March, and submitted it to IEEE Globecom just before 1 April. As the deadline came closer, I was often in the office for twelve hours a day, leaving at eight or nine in the evening. Good guidance can show you the way, but the reading, the debugging, the writing and the late evenings still have to be done by you.

I was also lucky in the people around me. My co-supervisor gave me direction and the right material at the very start. My day-to-day supervisor turned reading into real discussion and became the best collaborator I could have asked for. And my professor guided the bigger picture, including where our work would fit best. A first paper has one name at the front, but it is never built alone.

After submitting, I kept working on the same problem with a different approach, focused on making the solution more scalable. That became my second paper, which I submitted before August and which was accepted at IEEE CloudNet.

The Globecom decision, though, arrived in the most inconvenient way possible. In August, I flew home to India for the holidays, and the notification was due around midnight, right in the middle of an eight-hour flight with no internet. I landed at 2 a.m., and my SIM card refused to work. So I met my parents, got into the car, and borrowed my mom's hotspot. One email was waiting. I opened it, read the first line twice, and then turned to my parents to tell them. The paper was accepted.

My family has always celebrated every academic milestone of mine, sometimes a little dramatically, and this one was no exception. We ordered a small cake that said "25% doctor already", because one of the papers my PhD requires was done. Any researcher will tell you that's not quite how a PhD works. But it was our way of celebrating, and I wouldn't change it.

If you are working on your first paper, here is what I would share. Stay connected with your supervisors. They know the field far better than you do at the start, and they can point you to the papers and material that matter. Read review papers and state-of-the-art work before trying to find your own idea. Discuss what you read with someone, because gaps often appear in conversation rather than on the page. And be ready to put in the hours, especially near the deadline.

Seeing my name before "et al." for the first time was a small line on a page. But for me, it holds December's notes, February's conversations, March's late evenings and one long flight home. It is only the first one, and I am looking forward to the next.
